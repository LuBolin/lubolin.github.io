This week was about turning our pain point into actual questions. The feedback on our group milestone had a key question: why university students? Looking back, a big reason for picking the user group was because they were easy to reach. We are students, our friends are students, so recruiting is basically free. That is convenient, but it is not really a justification.

We spent a long time on this. Scoping up to everyone who uses Google Docs felt too broad, because that group includes writers, project managers, and people sharing a shopping list. A pain point broad enough to cover all of them would be too vague to redesign. Scoping down was also hard to pin down. A student writing a thesis has to move through long documents constantly, so the navigation problem would hit them hard. A student jotting quick notes probably never opens the outline. Same app, same demographic, but different needs, and whichever subgroup we picked would speak for the rest. In the end, we pushed the unsettled argument into the interview design instead.

We then compared survey against interview. A survey would tell us how many people feel frustrated, but an interview would tell us why, and in what situation. We ended up choosing a semi-structured interview, because what we are missing is context, not numbers. It also lets us chase a "why" when someone says something unexpected, which a survey cannot.

Actually writing the questions turned out to be more revealing than I expected. Our outline now branches: school work versus outside-school work, and each branch asks the whole thing again. This is us admitting that one "student" does not exist, so we ask about each context separately and let the data decide which one matters. There is also a question on whether the context of use is similar or different across mobile and PC, which tests last week's cross-device idea instead of repeating it. The navigation section branches too, on whether someone uses tabs, the outline, or neither. The "neither" path is the interesting one: why not? What stops you? In a way every "if no" question asks whether our pain point even exists for that person, which is a scary thing to build into an interview. Our first draft had overlapping questions, and trimming them forced us to explain what each question was for.

A key takeaway was realizing how many assumptions we have been carrying. We assumed mobile use means short, rushed sessions, and that losing your place while navigating is the main frustration. We also assumed students cross-reference and navigate more than other groups. Writing the questions made these assumptions explicit, so the interviews can check them. If the answers differ from what we expected, we should be ready to adjust our scope, or even let go of the pain point, rather than hold onto it just because we picked it early.
